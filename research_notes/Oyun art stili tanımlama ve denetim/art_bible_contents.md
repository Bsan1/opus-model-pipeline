# Art bible contents for a stylised 3D game: sections, studio rules, outsourcing practice, stylisation level

Research scope: what a production art bible / visual style guide contains for a stylised 3D game (props, environments, tools/vehicles), with concrete stated rules from known games and talks, so a code-first asset pipeline can mirror the structure in a generated "art bible".

Source-quality note for the report writer: GDC Vault slides/videos were mostly behind access prompts, and several primary pages were only reachable as press write-ups. Items marked "(search summary only)" were seen in search-result summaries but the page itself was not fetched and verified; treat them as lower confidence. Everything else was read from the fetched page.

## Q1. What sections does a game art bible / style guide contain?

### Takeaway
Public art bibles and practitioner guides converge on the same skeleton: (1) vision/theme and a tie-breaker rule, (2) shape language and silhouette, (3) proportions and scale, (4) colour/value rules (palettes, hue shifting, value ranges, hierarchy), (5) materials and texture language (including what is modelled vs painted), (6) lighting/post intent, (7) technical budgets (texture sizes / texel density, poly counts, naming, folders), and (8) do/don't and "repeat-the-important-rules" pages. The most useful public documents state rules as numbers and hard prohibitions (e.g. "never pure #000000 outlines", "nothing smaller than a hand is modelled"), not as adjectives.

### Cited Findings
**Public art bibles (concrete headings and rules)**
- 0 A.D. Art Design Document (Wildfire Games) has about 21 sections including artistic theme, concept art, models, skins and textures, animations, folder structure and file-naming conventions. — [Makko: What Four Public Game Art Bibles Actually Say](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/)
- 0 A.D. states an explicit tie-breaker: "Gameplay trumps Realism when the two topics disagree." — [Makko](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/)
- 0 A.D. scales texture size by gameplay importance, not object type: unit props 128, typical units 256, hero units 512, structures/terrain at the top of the ladder (exact terrain value not given in the write-up). — [Makko](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/)
- 0 A.D. detail floor: "Anything smaller than a human hand should not be modeled but included in texturing." — [Makko](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/)
- Liberated Pixel Cup Style Guide (OpenGameArt with Mozilla and FSF) ends with a section titled "Things that are important enough to be worth repeating": no pure colours, varied hue ramps, yellowish highlights, purplish shadows, block out before detailing. — [Makko](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/)
- LPC also fixes the camera (top-down about 60 degrees, orthographic), the grid (32x32 tiles, 16x16 sub-tiles), sprite bases (32x48, clothing 48x64), the drop shadow (#322125 at 60% opacity), light direction (from above, biased left), and outlines (a darker shade of the local colour, not black; characters black or near-black, no selective outlining). — [Makko](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/)
- Battle for Wesnoth "Creating Unit Art": 72x72 canvas, feet baseline about 55 px from top, attack animations 4 frames minimum / 6 optimal, defence 2 frames; pure #000000 is never used to outline a sprite; light comes from "the artist's (not the unit's!) upper right." — [Makko](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/)
- Riot Games League of Legends VFX Style Guide: avoid "the extremes of 0 or 100" in value and use "more of the mid-range to define your palette"; one element is primary (sets the focal point) and secondary elements support it through value and saturation; with complementary colours one must act as the secondary colour; effects must be "easily readable within gameplay." — [Makko](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/)
- The Makko write-up notes that none of the four documents treats materials or texel density as a named rule, and none has do/don't pages in a formal form; it also argues they all assume a human makes each asset and none address generated art. — [Makko](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/)

**Practitioner/vendor descriptions of typical sections**
- Typical contents listed in practitioner sources: palettes, line weights, shading methods, lighting philosophy; character anatomy, costume motifs, silhouettes and sizing; environment texture density, prop scale and material definitions; UI fonts, icons and HUD styling; technical constraints (poly budgets, texture sizes, memory limits, shader complexity). (search summary only) — [iXie Gaming: From Style Guide to Engine-Ready](https://www.ixiegaming.com/blog/high-performance-game-art-pipelines-from-style-guide-to-engine-ready/); [Zatun: 5 outsourcing mistakes](https://zatun.com/five-mistakes-that-could-kill-your-project/)
- Juego Studio recommends an art bible with shape language, proportion rules, palette and material logic, lighting intent, and do/don't examples an outsourced artist can follow without asking; it claims visual consistency does not hold on its own past about 30 to 40 assets and 2 to 3 artists. (vendor blog, search summary only; the 30-40 figure is unsourced opinion) — [Juego Studio: Art Direction in Games](https://www.juegostudio.com/blog/art-direction-in-games)
- An art bible is described on GameDev.net as a reference where art decisions are recorded so the team can check later what was decided and stay consistent. (search summary only) — [GameDev.net: what is an art bible](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/644435-what-is-an-art-bible-and-any-tips-to-make-one-better/)
- An academic taxonomy of game-development artifacts (UW iSchool) exists and lists art bibles among production documents (not fetched). — [Taxonomy of Video Game Development Artifacts v1.1 (PDF)](https://gamer.ischool.uw.edu/files/2019/09/Taxonomy-of-Video-Game-Development-Artifacts.v.1.1.pdf)

**Colour script / lighting section (Firewatch)**
- Firewatch used "A colour script, aligned to key moments in the narrative ... to ensure consistency throughout the game"; Jane Ng warned against a "crazy parade, birthday party colour scheme when you're trying to be spooky." — [Thumbsticks: GDC 2015 The Art of Firewatch](https://www.thumbsticks.com/gdc-2015-the-art-of-firewatch)
- "the biggest chunk of colour is actually determined by the sky"; lighting is "supplemented with the use of fog to create depth and distance"; colours can be changed "on the fly as the player moves around." — [Thumbsticks](https://www.thumbsticks.com/gdc-2015-the-art-of-firewatch)

**Texel density (technical section)**
- Maxon's Cinema 4D docs give per-camera texel density targets: first-person 10.24, third-person 5.12, top-down 2.56 or smaller (values match px/cm, i.e. 1024 / 512 / 256 px/m), and note that objects with a very different texel density from their surroundings stand out, so one consistent density matters most. — [Maxon C4D: Texel Density](https://help.maxon.net/c4d/en-us/Content/html/Texel_Density.html)
- An 80.lv hand-painted stylised workflow checks texel density during UV work but gives no target; the author keeps scenes under about 50,000 tris and textures "at the lowest resolution possible without losing details." (search summary only) — [80.lv: Studying hand-painted texturing workflow](https://80.lv/articles/studying-hand-painted-texturing-workflow-for-stylized-art)

### Inferences
- A generated art bible for a code-first pipeline can mirror this skeleton with one section per measurable rule family: 1 Vision + tie-breaker; 2 Shape language (primitive vocabulary, big/medium/small ratio, silhouette test); 3 Proportion and scale (1:1 reference heights, exaggeration factors); 4 Colour (palette swatches with hex, value range clamp e.g. no 0/100, hue-shift direction for shadows/highlights, primary/secondary hierarchy, saturation per object class); 5 Materials (material ball sheet: one sphere per registry material, with base colour, roughness, edge-highlight rule); 6 Texture language (painted vs procedural, texel density target, detail floor "what is not modelled"); 7 Lighting/post (key light direction, fog/sky colour keys, colour script per time of day); 8 Technical budgets (tri counts, texture sizes by gameplay importance, naming); 9 Do/don't sheet; 10 "Rules worth repeating" summary page (LPC pattern).
- The LPC "worth repeating" page and the 0 A.D. tie-breaker are cheap to replicate and map directly onto automated checks (gates) in a pipeline.
- Material ball / material sphere sheets, turnarounds and prop sheets are standard practice but no fetched primary source in this pass described them in detail (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the original 0 A.D., LPC, Wesnoth or Riot VFX documents directly; all their rules above come from the Makko summary (which gives no URLs). The originals should be checked before quoting numbers verbatim.
- No primary source found that shows a real studio's material-ball sheet, turnaround sheet or prop sheet layout for a stylised 3D game; these remain "known practice" without a citation here.
- No published stylised/hand-painted texel density standard (px/m) found from a studio.

## Q2. How do known stylised games describe their style, and what exact rules did they state?

### Takeaway
The stated rules cluster around: simplify form ("simplify everything", "no tiny bumps or textile patterns", "flat shapes with abstract internal details"), deliberate wonkiness / no parallel lines, painted rather than photo-sourced textures, visible use and repair, edge lightening on convex edges, characters always reading against environment, a single heavily styled light, sky/fog driving most of the colour, and a tie-breaker that readability/gameplay beats realism. Several famous names in the brief (Hades, Ori, A Short Hike, Animal Crossing, Tunic, cozy farming games) yielded no citable primary art-rule statements in this pass.

### Cited Findings
**Sea of Thieves (Rare; GDC 2018 "Visual Adventures on Sea of Thieves", art director Ryan Stevenson)**
- Talk exists and is free on the GDC YouTube channel; it covered "a few simple rules [that] permeated through all aspects of a game's visuals." — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/art/video-how-rare-crafted-the-look-and-feel-of-i-sea-of-thieves-i-); [YouTube talk link via Habrador](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BzppoQTG3m0)
- Rules as summarised by Habrador: "The graphics should not be realistic, so you have to simplify everything"; complex scenes come from combining several simple objects; photos cannot be used directly as textures, so photo reference is simplified by hand (shipped textures ended up more detailed than the early painted studies but still stylised, e.g. palm leaves); "Use the light source to change the mode"; "The stuff you add should look like it has been used" (a shovel dented from hitting rock, a gun patched with a leather strap); forms should be "wonky" but realistically so. — [Habrador: stylized graphics Fortnite / Sea of Thieves](https://blog.habrador.com/2018/08/stylized-graphics-fortnite-sea-of-thieves.html)
- Critic framing: "caricature-like" quality, "breathtaking in its simplicity." (search summary only) — [Shacknews best art style 2018](https://shacknews.com/article/109011/shacknews-best-art-style-of-2018-sea-of-thieves?amphtml=1)

**Fortnite (Epic; GDC 2018 "Developing the Art of Fortnite", art director Peter Ellis)**
- Talk and speaker confirmed; it traced the style from early drab, monotone environments to brighter, more colourful work and covered rendering the stylised art in UE4. — [GDC: Attend GDC 2018 ... art design of Fortnite](https://www.gdconf.com/news/attend-gdc-2018-and-get-an-epic-look-at-the-art-design-of-fortnite/); [Game Developer video](https://gamedeveloper.com/art/video-developing-the-stylized-art-of-i-fortnite-i-)
- Rules as summarised by Habrador: realistic textures simplified in Photoshop came out too noisy, so textures were hand-painted to add character; forms are deformed in a cartoonish way by removing all parallel lines from a real-world object (photo reference can stand in for concept art for this); normal and specular maps are still used; add wear, dust and leaky bolts for character; nothing smaller than a mailbox (small objects slow players), grass allowed as non-colliding decoration. — [Habrador](https://blog.habrador.com/2018/08/stylized-graphics-fortnite-sea-of-thieves.html); [YouTube talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=498KToofNf8)

**Overwatch (Blizzard; GDC 2017 "The Art of Overwatch", Bill Petras and Arnold Tsang)**
- GDC Vault lists the talk; coverage says it set early art-direction points such as diversity, a hopeful future and a vibrant world, and that Petras described a handcrafted look for materials combined with exaggerated hero silhouettes, driven by gameplay readability. (search summary only; quote not verified on a fetched page) — [GDC Vault: The Art of Overwatch](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024268/The-Art-of-Overwatch-Evolving); [Vice: what it's like to art direct Overwatch](https://creators.vice.com/en_us/article/heres-what-its-like-to-art-direct-overwatch); [Cook & Becker: Designing Overwatch](https://cookandbecker.com/en/article/378/designing-overwatch.html)
- Shape-to-role logic: tanks larger, blockier or rounder; fast precision heroes tighter, smaller, thinner; concept artist notes squares read sturdy and circles harmless. (search summary only) — [Blizzard concept artist post via devtrackers](https://devtrackers.gg/overwatch/p/004a3f0d-what-it-s-like-to-work-on-overwatch-as-a-concept-artist)
- Third-party analysis of Overwatch weapons (Joe Maleski, 80.lv, not official Blizzard guidance): models are "recognizable by only their silhouette"; build large silhouette forms "before going near the small details"; Blizzard artists "really don't get too hyper detailed with the meshes" and "you don't find a lot of tiny bumps or textile patterns"; they "lighten the base color along convex edges of shapes" (recreated in Substance with a lightened base-layer copy masked by a scratches smart mask); "Almost every metal is going to have at least a bit of imperfection in it keeping it from being perfectly shiny." — [80.lv: Studying Overwatch Style](https://80.lv/articles/studying-overwatch-style)

**VALORANT (Riot)**
- Riot's tech blog names three pillars: "performance, competitive integrity, and art"; "characters need to always stand out from their environment"; "we use a single light and style the heck out of it to make it look good"; "we want to stray away from the closer-to-reality physics of light interaction"; "Our artists paint hotspots on our static HDR images to drive the shape and behavior of the highlight they want"; "characters don't need to be shiny to read well"; skin subsurface faked with stylised underglow and gradients; "we don't have dynamic shadows in the playable space of the game"; max darkness/brightness is tuned per map area; enemies get a red fresnel and allies a neutral blue one; vision-blocking effects have "a harsh geometric gameplay edge"; frame budget 6.9 ms for 144 FPS. — [Riot: VALORANT Shaders and Gameplay Clarity](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/valorant-shaders-and-gameplay-clarity) (mirror of [technology.riotgames.com/node/110](https://technology.riotgames.com/node/110))
- Art director Moby Francke called the style "illustrative visual design", a balance between realism and cel-shaded animation, with accessibility/readability across hardware prioritised over beauty ("We're not trying to make the most beautiful game ever"). (search summary only) — [Inverse interview with Moby Francke](https://inverse.com/gaming/valorant-art-style-interview-moby-francke)
- Weapon muzzle-flash VFX were designed to differ by weapon shape and power so players can read the gun. (search summary only) — [VALORANT: How the arsenal was built](https://playvalorant.com/en-us/news/dev/how-the-valorant-arsenal-was-built/)

**Breath of the Wild (Nintendo; GDC 2017, art director Satoru Takizawa)**
- Team searched for a style where it is "easy to lie" while keeping "a feeling of reality"; Wind Waker's style "told lies that were too big", so subtler deception was needed; art should "intuitively suggest possible physics and chemistry gameplay"; the team needed "an information-dense approach and a certain level of realism" while keeping "clarity and colour"; art team slogan "Refreshing and full-flavoured"; some elements "deliberately avoided realism" (broken logs instantly become tied firewood; food bounces in the pot like rubber balls). — [Thumbsticks: GDC17 Designing Zelda BotW](https://www.thumbsticks.com/gdc17-designing-zelda-breath-of-the-wild/)
- Wind Waker HD mock-ups "stood out" and captured the lead artists' imagination; Master Works framed the style as "intentional contraction of reality" with comedic touches. (search summary only) — [Zelda Universe](https://zeldauniverse.net/2017/03/14/takizawa-explains-how-the-wind-waker-hd-inspired-the-visual-style-for-breath-of-the-wild/)

**Firewatch (Campo Santo; GDC 2015 "The Art of Firewatch", Jane Ng)**
- "Much of the composition is made with flat-shapes with strong, distinctive silhouettes with abstract internal details"; "bold colours ... in very distinct layers. Each layer adds to a feeling of depth and distance"; colour layers "really do drive the mood of the scene"; style derived from Olly Moss's posters; "any object with a certain level of texture detail is something that adds to your story"; "Embrace your limitations." — [Thumbsticks: GDC 2015 The Art of Firewatch](https://www.thumbsticks.com/gdc-2015-the-art-of-firewatch)
- The talk's GDC listing frames it as translating a bold 2D graphic style into an explorable 3D environment; a separate talk, "Making the World of Firewatch", is a production postmortem (scene management, asset modelling, streaming). — [GDC Vault: The Art of Firewatch](https://gdcvault.com/play/1022295/The-Art-of); [Boing Boing coverage](https://boingboing.net/2015/03/27/how-a-small-art-team-created-f.html)

### Inferences
- Rules that recur across at least two studios and are directly checkable in a procedural pipeline: (a) silhouette-first / large forms before details (Overwatch, Firewatch); (b) no fine surface noise in geometry, detail goes to paint or is omitted (Overwatch, 0 A.D., Fortnite's "too noisy"); (c) break straight/parallel lines with controlled wonkiness (Fortnite, Sea of Thieves); (d) visible use, wear and repair as storytelling (Sea of Thieves, Fortnite); (e) readable focal object vs environment contrast (VALORANT, LoL VFX); (f) one key light plus sky/fog driving colour (VALORANT, Firewatch); (g) texture detail concentrated where it matters (Firewatch story objects, 0 A.D. importance ladder).
- Convex edge lightening (Overwatch analysis) and painted HDR highlights (VALORANT) are both "edge/highlight treatment" rules; an art bible should give edge treatment its own section with a numeric lightening amount.
- For a garden/mowing game, the Fortnite "nothing smaller than a mailbox" rule translates to a minimum collidable-prop size, and the BotW "lies not too big" framing is a useful one-line stylisation target.

### Gaps
- No primary or verified art-rule statements found in this pass for Hades (Supergiant/Jen Zee), Ori, A Short Hike, Animal Crossing, Tunic, Ghibli-like games or cozy farming/garden games. A search on Tunic returned only a Finji newsletter about Andrew Shouldice's notebooks and the TUNIC: Design Works book, with no texture/colour rules ([Finji November update](https://finji.co/news/2025/11/21/NovemberUpdate.html)).
- The original Fortnite and Sea of Thieves slides were not reachable; the rules come from Habrador's summary of the YouTube recordings. The talks themselves should be watched to confirm wording.
- Overwatch rules come from a third-party artist's analysis (80.lv) and search summaries; no official Blizzard written style rule (e.g. "big-medium-small", edge-highlight percentages) was verified.
- No verified source for Overwatch/Blizzard "hand-painted vs PBR" split, or for any studio's numeric colour keys (hex palettes) other than the LPC shadow colour.

## Q3. What do GDC / art-direction talks recommend for writing a style guide that outsourcing partners can follow? Do/don't examples.

### Takeaway
The consistent message is that vendors execute well but do not do art direction, so the client must front-load the design: a style guide first, then per-batch briefs that are highly specific, with poor results usually traced to weak briefs. Vague briefs ("Movie One meets Movie Two") and missing specs are the classic failure; do/don't pages and rules specific enough to need no extra briefing are the recommended remedy.

### Cited Findings
- Guerrilla Games (GDC 2016 Art Direction Bootcamp, Maarten van der Gaag, "Guerrilla Games' Approach to Asset Production") covers its asset pipeline and how its "highly detailed briefs" are made; Guerrilla keeps a very small internal asset team and a relatively large concept team, so it needs a mature outsourcing pipeline. — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1023575/Art-Direction-Bootcamp-Guerrilla-Games); [GDC Vault (alt)](https://gdcvault.com/play/1023158/contactUs)
- Reported pipeline timings: style guide about 2 months; briefs about 1 month per batch; touch-ups about 1 week per batch; outsourcing about 2 months; integration 1 to 2 weeks; about 6 months total. Style guides matter more for a new IP such as Horizon Zero Dawn; outsourced artists execute well but do not handle visual design or art direction; even junior staff can write high-quality briefs with the right preparation; poor results are usually attributed to the originating studio's briefs, not the vendor. Concept team had 17 concept artists (7 permanent). Killzone: Shadow Fall reportedly had about 14,000 art assets, mostly from 100+ outsourced artists. — [DualShockers report on the talk](https://www.dualshockers.com/ps4-exclusive-horizon-zero-dawns-new-images-show-a-roof-guerrilla-talks-outsourcing/)
- Guerrilla art directors give vendors feedback daily, with an outsourcing producer handling long-term planning and contracts. (search summary only) — [80.lv: Outsourcing Producer at Guerrilla](https://80.lv/articles/game-jobs-outsourcing-producer-at-guerrilla)
- GDC Game Outsourcing Summit "When does it work?": unclear art guidance and specifications are among the main don'ts; in one case a non-realistic style looked great in samples but the vendor got no specific art direction, and a poor written spec with little support led to many disconnects; vague comparisons like "Movie One meets Movie Two" worked for one team but not another; a direct relationship with the vendor is simple and effective. (search summary only) — [Game Developer: GDC Game Outsourcing Summit](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-game-outsourcing-summit-when-does-it-work-)
- Do not outsource when there is no clear creative vision, because an outsourcing company cannot be relied on to work out the style for you. (search summary only) — [Game Developer: Five reasons not to outsource art](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/art/five-reasons-not-to-outsource-art)
- Vendor guidance: write the art bible before looking for partners, pair it with a technical bible for asset requirements, use a reusable briefing template, and review milestones in-engine; vendors otherwise fill gaps with their own interpretation of vague briefs. (vendor blogs, search summary only) — [Zatun: 5 outsourcing mistakes](https://zatun.com/five-mistakes-that-could-kill-your-project/); [iXie Gaming](https://www.ixiegaming.com/blog/high-performance-game-art-pipelines-from-style-guide-to-engine-ready/)
- Concrete do/don't style rules from public guides (phrased as prohibitions): never pure #000000 outlines (Wesnoth); no pure colours, shadows purple-shifted, highlights yellow-shifted (LPC); avoid value extremes 0 and 100 (Riot LoL VFX); do not model anything smaller than a hand (0 A.D.). — [Makko](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/)
- Firewatch don't: no "crazy parade, birthday party colour scheme when you're trying to be spooky" (colour script must follow narrative mood). — [Thumbsticks](https://www.thumbsticks.com/gdc-2015-the-art-of-firewatch)
- Fortnite don't: do not use photo textures simplified in Photoshop (too noisy); don't keep parallel lines; no collidable props smaller than a mailbox. — [Habrador](https://blog.habrador.com/2018/08/stylized-graphics-fortnite-sea-of-thieves.html)

### Inferences
- The Guerrilla split (style guide once, then per-batch briefs) maps onto a pipeline as: a project-level art bible (generated once from the style definition) plus a per-asset brief (generated per model: references, scale, materials used from the registry, detail floor, budget, do/don't specific to that asset type).
- Since vendors (or AI generators) "fill gaps with their own interpretation", every adjective in a generated art bible should be backed by a number or a checkable prohibition; the public guides that are most reusable are exactly the ones phrased that way.
- Do/don't pages work best when each "don't" is a specific observed failure, which matches the project's existing rule that each defect found by eye becomes a gate, test or rule.

### Gaps
- No fetched source showed an actual do/don't page layout (image pairs with captions) from a stylised 3D studio; Guerrilla's slides and brief templates were not accessible.
- The Juego Studio claim that consistency breaks down past about 30-40 assets / 2-3 artists is an unsourced vendor opinion.

## Q4. How is "stylisation level" defined and communicated (exaggeration, simplification, modelled vs painted vs omitted)?

### Takeaway
No source found defines stylisation as a numeric "exaggeration %". Studios communicate it instead through (1) a reference anchor ("Wind Waker HD but with smaller lies"; "Olly Moss posters in 3D"; "illustrative visual design between realism and cel-shading"), (2) a tie-breaker ("gameplay trumps realism"), and (3) concrete thresholds for what is modelled vs painted vs omitted ("smaller than a human hand is painted, not modelled"; "nothing smaller than a mailbox"; "no tiny bumps or textile patterns"; texture detail only on story objects).

### Cited Findings
- BotW framed stylisation as degree of "lying": Wind Waker's lies were "too big"; the target kept "a feeling of reality" while being "easy to lie"; moving away from realness improves tempo and visibility but the world's reality "is also apt to be lost." — [Thumbsticks: GDC17 BotW](https://www.thumbsticks.com/gdc17-designing-zelda-breath-of-the-wild/)
- VALORANT's level is stated as a position between two poles: "illustrative visual design", a balance between realism and cel-shaded animation. (search summary only) — [Inverse](https://inverse.com/gaming/valorant-art-style-interview-moby-francke)
- Modelled vs painted threshold: "Anything smaller than a human hand should not be modeled but included in texturing" (0 A.D.). — [Makko](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/)
- Omission threshold for gameplay geometry: nothing smaller than a mailbox; grass only as non-colliding decoration (Fortnite). — [Habrador](https://blog.habrador.com/2018/08/stylized-graphics-fortnite-sea-of-thieves.html)
- Mesh-detail ceiling: "don't get too hyper detailed with the meshes"; few "tiny bumps or textile patterns" (Overwatch, third-party analysis). — [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/studying-overwatch-style)
- Simplification method: "simplify everything" and build complexity by combining simple objects; photo reference simplified by hand, not used directly (Sea of Thieves); remove parallel lines from real objects (Fortnite). — [Habrador](https://blog.habrador.com/2018/08/stylized-graphics-fortnite-sea-of-thieves.html)
- Abstraction of internal detail: "flat-shapes with strong, distinctive silhouettes with abstract internal details"; texture detail level itself signals story relevance (Firewatch). — [Thumbsticks: Firewatch](https://www.thumbsticks.com/gdc-2015-the-art-of-firewatch)
- Shading departs from physical accuracy by design: VALORANT "stray[s] away from the closer-to-reality physics of light interaction", fakes subsurface with gradients, and drops character specular. — [Riot](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/valorant-shaders-and-gameplay-clarity)
- Practitioner stylisation principles: exaggerated proportions and avoiding unnecessary detail (Lynda 3ds Max stylised environment course, presented as personal experience, not universal rules); the big/medium/small distribution of shapes, credited to Neil Blevins' tutorials and popularised by Gleb Alexandrov, is a composition heuristic rather than a formal standard. (search summaries only) — [Lynda: Elements of stylization](https://origin-www.lynda.com/3ds-Max-tutorials/Elements-stylization/503885/573623-4.html); [BlenderNation: Distribution of Shapes Theory](https://www.blendernation.com/2021/06/05/distribution-of-shapes-theory-for-better-renders/)
- Shape-meaning conventions used in shape-language sections: circles friendly/harmless, squares stable/sturdy, triangles aggressive. (general practice, search summary only) — [RocketBrush: shape language](https://rocketbrush.com/blog/shape-language-in-game-character-design-how-to-make-characters-readable-and-consistent); [80.lv: shape language and readability](https://80.lv/articles/character-design-shape-language-and-readability/)

### Inferences
- A code-first art bible can make "stylisation level" explicit and checkable with a small set of parameters: (1) anchor references (one or two named games/images plus "how far from them"); (2) detail floor in metres below which features are painted, and a second floor below which they are omitted (0 A.D. hand-size rule as precedent); (3) minimum gameplay-prop size (Fortnite mailbox precedent); (4) proportion exaggeration factors per part class (e.g. handles, wheels, blades thicker than real), stated as multipliers against 1:1 reference measurements; (5) straight-line breaking amount (wonkiness angle/offset ranges); (6) bevel/edge-highlight width and lightening amount; (7) surface noise allowance (how much high-frequency detail is permitted in textures).
- Multipliers and thresholds are an inference by this researcher; no studio source states stylisation as percentages, so the generated document should present them as project-defined numbers, not as industry standards.

### Gaps
- No source found that defines stylisation level as an "exaggeration %" or a numbered scale; also no studio-published "modelled / painted / omitted" table beyond the 0 A.D. rule and Fortnite's gameplay-size rule.
- No primary studio source found for the "big / medium small" ratio as an official art-bible rule (only practitioner tutorials and courses).
